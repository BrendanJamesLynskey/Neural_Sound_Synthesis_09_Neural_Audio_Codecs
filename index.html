Neural Audio Codecs

A neural codec is an autoencoder with a discrete bottleneck. It does two jobs at once: it crushes audio to a handful of kilobits per second with startling fidelity, and it turns a continuous waveform into a short sequence of integers — tokens a language model can read. This is the hinge of the whole series: everything before it is signal, everything after it is text. Three live demos build the machinery from vector quantization up to a full SoundStream / EnCodec pipeline.

1The discrete bottleneck — one architecture, two payoffs

Part 8 built the variational autoencoder: an encoder $E$ that maps audio $\mathbf{x}$ to a continuous latent $\mathbf{z}$, and a decoder $G$ that reconstructs it. A neural audio codec keeps that skeleton but forces the latent through a discrete gate — every frame is snapped to one of a finite set of learned vectors. That single change buys two things a continuous VAE cannot give you at once.

Compression. A discrete code is just an integer index. Send indices instead of floats and you get a bitstream — audio at 1.5–12 kbps that still sounds like speech or music, beating hand-built codecs like Opus at the very low end.
Tokenization. A finite alphabet of indices is a vocabulary. The waveform becomes a sentence of tokens, and a transformer can model $p(\text{token}_t \mid \text{token}_{

The pipeline is deliberately asymmetric to the generative model that will sit on top: the codec is trained once, offline, to reconstruct audio as faithfully as possible; the language model is trained separately over the tokens it produces. Get the codec right and the hard problem of Part 1 — audio is enormous, every sample entangled — collapses into a sequence-modelling problem no harder than language.

$$\mathbf{x} \;\xrightarrow{\;E\;}\; \mathbf{z} \;\xrightarrow{\;Q\;}\; \big[q_1,q_2,\dots,q_{N_q}\big] \;\xrightarrow{\;G\;}\; \hat{\mathbf{x}}, \qquad q_i \in \{1,\dots,K\}$$

The middle arrow, quantization $Q$, is the whole story. It is where the fidelity is lost, where the bitrate is set, and where the tokens are born. The rest of this part is an unpacking of that one operator — first for a single codebook (VQ), then for a stack of them (RVQ), then wrapped in the convolutional encoder/decoder and adversarial training that make SoundStream, EnCodec and DAC.

2Vector quantization — snapping a vector to its nearest word

The discrete gate is vector quantization, introduced to deep learning by the VQ-VAE (van den Oord et al., 2017). Hold a codebook — a learned table of $K$ vectors $\{\mathbf{e}_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$, each the same dimension as the encoder output. To quantize an encoder vector $\mathbf{z}_e$, find the nearest codebook entry and pass its index forward; the decoder reads back that entry $\mathbf{e}_k$.

$$k^\star = \arg\min_{k}\;\lVert \mathbf{z}_e - \mathbf{e}_k \rVert_2, \qquad \mathbf{z}_q = \mathbf{e}_{k^\star}$$

The problem: you cannot differentiate an $\arg\min$

Nearest-neighbour lookup is a step function — its gradient is zero almost everywhere and undefined at the boundaries. Back-propagation would have no signal to train the encoder. The VQ-VAE's trick is the straight-through estimator: on the forward pass use the quantized $\mathbf{z}_q$; on the backward pass simply copy the gradient from $\mathbf{z}_q$ straight onto $\mathbf{z}_e$, as if quantization were the identity.

$$\mathbf{z}_q = \mathbf{z}_e + \operatorname{sg}\!\big[\mathbf{e}_{k^\star} - \mathbf{z}_e\big], \qquad \frac{\partial \mathbf{z}_q}{\partial \mathbf{z}_e} \approx \mathbf{I}$$

where $\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]$ is the stop-gradient operator (identity forward, zero derivative backward). The forward value is exactly $\mathbf{e}_{k^\star}$; the backward value pretends the codebook was transparent. It is biased, but it works.

The losses that move the codebook

Straight-through trains the encoder and decoder, but the codebook vectors themselves get no gradient from reconstruction. Two extra terms fix that. The codebook loss pulls each chosen entry toward the encoder output; the commitment loss pushes the encoder output toward its chosen entry so it does not wander between codes.

$$\mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\lVert \mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}} \rVert^2}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;+\; \underbrace{\big\lVert \operatorname{sg}[\mathbf{z}_e] - \mathbf{e} \big\rVert^2}_{\text{codebook}} \;+\; \beta\,\underbrace{\big\lVert \mathbf{z}_e - \operatorname{sg}[\mathbf{e}] \big\rVert^2}_{\text{commitment}}$$

The stop-gradients split the responsibility: the codebook term only moves $\mathbf{e}$, the commitment term only moves $\mathbf{z}_e$, and $\beta\approx0.25$ balances how tightly the encoder is held to the table. In practice the codebook term is usually replaced by an exponential moving average (EMA) update — treat each entry as the running mean of the encoder vectors that selected it, which is more stable than an SGD step.

$$\mathbf{e}_k \leftarrow \frac{\sum_i \mathbb{1}[k_i=k]\,\mathbf{z}_{e,i}}{\sum_i \mathbb{1}[k_i=k]}\quad\text{(as a decaying EMA over batches)}$$
Codebook collapse. The pathology that haunts VQ: a few entries win every lookup and the rest are never selected, so most of the vocabulary is dead and the effective bitrate is far below $\log_2 K$. Cures include EMA updates, re-initialising dead codes to busy encoder outputs (random restarts), lower code dimension, and L2-normalising both codes and encoder outputs so the $\arg\min$ becomes a cosine lookup — the DAC recipe in Section 6.

derivation · why the straight-through estimator is the right bias

Write the encoder as $\mathbf{z}_e=E_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ and the reconstruction loss as $\ell(\hat{\mathbf{x}})=\ell(G(\mathbf{z}_q))$. The exact gradient $\partial \ell/\partial\theta$ needs $\partial\mathbf{z}_q/\partial\mathbf{z}_e$, which is zero: perturbing $\mathbf{z}_e$ within a Voronoi cell leaves $k^\star$, hence $\mathbf{z}_q$, unchanged. The straight-through estimator replaces that Jacobian with the identity, giving the surrogate

$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta} \approx \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mathbf{z}_q}\,\frac{\partial \mathbf{z}_e}{\partial \theta}.$$

This is exactly correct in the limit of a fine codebook, where $\mathbf{z}_q\to\mathbf{z}_e$ and quantization is nearly the identity. For a coarse codebook it is biased, but the bias points the encoder in a direction that reduces reconstruction error for the code it actually selected — the only thing we can act on. The commitment term then shrinks $\lVert\mathbf{z}_e-\mathbf{e}_{k^\star}\rVert$, making the approximation progressively tighter during training.

3Residual VQ — a stack of codebooks refining the leftover

A single codebook of $K$ entries carries only $\log_2 K$ bits per frame. To reach the fidelity a codec needs you would want an enormous $K$ — but a million-entry codebook is impossible to train (collapse) and slow to search. Residual vector quantization sidesteps this: quantize coarsely with codebook 1, take the residual — what the first code missed — and quantize that with codebook 2, and so on down a stack of $N_q$ codebooks.

$$\mathbf{r}_0 = \mathbf{z}_e,\qquad q_i = \arg\min_{k}\lVert \mathbf{r}_{i-1}-\mathbf{e}^{(i)}_k\rVert,\qquad \mathbf{r}_i = \mathbf{r}_{i-1}-\mathbf{e}^{(i)}_{q_i}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{z}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N_q}\mathbf{e}^{(i)}_{q_i}$$

Each stage chips away at the error the previous stages left behind, so the residual magnitude $\lVert\mathbf{r}_i\rVert$ shrinks geometrically and the reconstruction $\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ marches toward the true encoder vector. With $N_q$ codebooks of $K$ entries each, the effective vocabulary is $K^{N_q}$ — the reach of a giant codebook — at the training cost and search cost of $N_q$ small ones.

Left: the input (★) reached by a chain of chosen codewords, one per stage. Right: the residual magnitude collapsing stage by stage. This is the heart of SoundStream / EnCodec.
Fine bitrate control. Because the codebooks are ordered coarse→fine, you can truncate the stack at decode time: keep the first $n\le N_q$ codes and you have a valid, lower-bitrate reconstruction. One trained codec serves a whole ladder of bitrates.
Quantizer dropout. To make that truncation actually work, SoundStream trains with a random number of active stages per step — so every prefix of the stack is optimised to reconstruct on its own, not just the full depth.
derivation · why residual magnitude decays geometrically

Suppose each codebook's entries are spaced so that the nearest one lands within a ball of radius proportional to the current residual's scale — i.e. stage $i$ reduces the residual norm by a roughly constant factor $\rho<1$:

$$\lVert\mathbf{r}_i\rVert \le \rho\,\lVert\mathbf{r}_{i-1}\rVert \;\Rightarrow\; \lVert\mathbf{r}_{N_q}\rVert \le \rho^{\,N_q}\,\lVert\mathbf{z}_e\rVert.$$

The error falls exponentially in the number of stages, so distortion in dB is linear in $N_q$: every added codebook buys a roughly constant SNR increment, exactly the behaviour Demo 5 plots for real audio. The factor $\rho$ improves as each codebook is trained to tile its own residual distribution — which is why the per-stage codebooks are learned jointly with the encoder, not fixed.

4SoundStream & EnCodec — the full pipeline

SoundStream (Zeghidour et al., 2021) and EnCodec (Défossez et al., 2022) wrap RVQ in a fully convolutional, streaming encoder/decoder and train the whole thing end-to-end with a mixture of reconstruction and adversarial objectives. The result is a real-time neural codec that runs on a laptop CPU. Hover the blocks below to walk the signal from waveform to tokens and back.

Hover a block to inspect it. Raw audio → convolutional encoder (downsampling) → RVQ bottleneck (the discrete tokens) → convolutional decoder (upsampling) → audio.

The four pieces

  • Convolutional encoder. A cascade of strided 1-D convolutions with residual blocks. Each strided layer downsamples in time (strides like $2,4,5,8$), so ~$24\,000$ samples per second collapse to ~$75$ latent frames per second — the total stride is the product, e.g. $320\times$.
  • RVQ bottleneck. Each latent frame is quantized by the stack of Section 3 into $N_q$ integer codes. These codes are the transmitted bitstream and the tokens Part 10 models.
  • Convolutional decoder. A mirror of the encoder using transposed convolutions that upsample back to the waveform rate.
  • Streaming. All convolutions are causal and padding is cached, so the codec runs frame-by-frame with a fixed, small latency — essential for live calls.

The training objective — reconstruct, and fool a critic

An $\ell_2$ waveform loss alone gives muffled audio (it averages away phase detail, exactly the Part 1 lesson). Neural codecs instead combine three ingredients, and it is the combination that yields fidelity.

TermWhat it doesForm
Multi-scale STFT Match magnitude spectrograms at several window sizes — captures both fine pitch and broad spectral shape. $\sum_s \lVert |\text{STFT}_s(\mathbf{x})| - |\text{STFT}_s(\hat{\mathbf{x}})|\rVert_1$
Adversarial Multiple discriminators (multi-scale & multi-period, as in HiFi-GAN of Part 3) push $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ onto the manifold of real audio, restoring crispness a regression loss blurs. $\min_G\max_D\ \mathbb{E}[\log D(\mathbf{x})] + \mathbb{E}[\log(1-D(\hat{\mathbf{x}}))]$
VQ commitment The Section 2 commitment loss on every RVQ stage, keeping encoder outputs married to their codes. $\sum_i \lVert \mathbf{r}_{i-1} - \operatorname{sg}[\mathbf{e}^{(i)}_{q_i}]\rVert_2^2$

A feature-matching loss (match the discriminator's internal activations for real vs fake) usually joins them to stabilise the GAN. EnCodec adds a small entropy-coding stage — an arithmetic coder over the code indices — that squeezes a further ~25 % off the bitrate for free, since the codes are not uniformly used.

Streaming vs quality. Making the convolutions causal costs a little fidelity (the decoder cannot look ahead) but is what lets EnCodec run in real time inside a video call. A non-causal variant, trained the same way, scores higher when latency does not matter — the classic codec trade-off, now learned rather than hand-tuned.

5Bitrate & quality — hear the codebooks add up

The bitrate of an RVQ codec is a single tidy product: the number of frames per second, times the number of codebooks, times the bits per codebook. Nothing else. That is what makes RVQ so controllable — every knob in the formula is an integer you can dial.

$$\text{bits/s} \;=\; f_r \times N_q \times \log_2 K$$

With EnCodec's $f_r=75$ frames/s, $K=1024$ (10 bits) and $N_q=8$ codebooks you get $75\times8\times10 = 6000$ bits/s $= 6$ kbps; drop to $N_q=2$ and you are at $1.5$ kbps — near-telephone quality from 1500 bits a second. Because the stack is ordered, changing $N_q$ needs no retraining: you just transmit fewer codes.

6DAC — closing the codebook-usage gap

The Descript Audio Codec (Kumar et al., 2023) took the SoundStream/EnCodec template and fixed its weakest link: codebook collapse. EnCodec's codebooks were often only partly used, wasting bitrate. DAC's changes push utilisation close to 100 % and let a single model compress 44.1 kHz audio — speech, music, environmental sound — at ~8 kbps with quality that beats EnCodec at 3× the rate.

The three key improvements

  • Factorized, L2-normalized codes. The encoder output is projected to a low-dimensional space (e.g. 8-D) for the lookup, and both the code and the query are L2-normalized so nearest-neighbour becomes a cosine search on a hypersphere. Low dimension + normalization dramatically raises how many codes get used.
  • Snake activation. Borrowed from BigVGAN, the periodic activation $x + \tfrac{1}{\alpha}\sin^2(\alpha x)$ gives the decoder a built-in inductive bias toward periodic (i.e. pitched, harmonic) signals — a better fit for audio than ReLU.
  • Quantizer dropout, fixed. DAC noted that naïve quantizer dropout slightly hurts full-bitrate quality and applies it more carefully, keeping the bitrate-scalability benefit without the fidelity tax.
$$\text{snake}(x) = x + \frac{1}{\alpha}\sin^2(\alpha x)$$

The lesson is general: the bottleneck was never the RVQ idea but the statistics of the codebook. Force the codes onto a sphere in a low-dimensional space and the $\arg\min$ spreads its mass, so the bits you paid for are actually spent. DAC is now the default codec behind many 2024–25 audio language models.

A neural codec is judged by a strange double standard: it must be a great compressor (low bitrate, high fidelity) and a great tokenizer (codes a language model can predict). DAC's high codebook usage helps both — every token carries information, so the downstream transformer never wastes capacity on dead symbols.

7Why discretization unlocks language models

Everything in this part has been building one object: a sequence of integers that stands in for a waveform. Once audio is a sequence of tokens over a fixed vocabulary, the entire machinery of language modelling applies unchanged — and that is the subject of Part 10.

A transformer models $p(\text{token}_t \mid \text{token}_{

The RVQ flattening problem. RVQ gives $N_q$ codes per frame, so the token grid is 2-D (time × codebook), not a 1-D stream. Part 10's whole craft is how to serialise it: AudioLM's coarse-to-fine stages, MusicGen's delayed-interleaving pattern, or predicting all $N_q$ codes of a frame in parallel. The codec sets the board; the language model chooses the move order.

Contrast with classical codecs

Classical codecs — MP3, AAC, Opus — also compress audio hard, but by hand-designed signal processing: an MDCT into the frequency domain, a psychoacoustic model that discards what the ear cannot hear, then entropy coding. They are superb compressors and terrible tokenizers — their bitstream is a tangle of scale factors and Huffman codes with no learnable, model-friendly structure. See the DSP & Music collection's MDCT guide for how the classical side works.

Neither replaces the other: Opus still wins on CPU cost and guaranteed low latency for a phone call. But only the neural codec doubles as a generative substrate, and that is why the field adopted it wholesale for synthesis.

8From VQ-VAE to DAC in six years

The discrete-bottleneck idea arrived for images and speech, then was engineered into a real-time audio compressor in a remarkably tight arc.

2017
VQ-VAE (van den Oord et al.) introduces the learned discrete codebook, the straight-through estimator, and the commitment loss — discretization as a differentiable layer. Part 8 · 9
2019
VQ-VAE-2 shows hierarchical codebooks scale to high-resolution images, foreshadowing multi-level audio codes.
2021
SoundStream (Zeghidour et al.) fuses a streaming conv encoder/decoder, residual VQ, and adversarial + STFT losses into the first real-time neural audio codec, with quantizer dropout for bitrate scalability. Part 9
2022
EnCodec (Défossez et al.) refines the recipe — multi-scale discriminators, a balancer for the loss mixture, and entropy coding — and ships as an open, high-quality 24/48 kHz codec. AudioLM starts modelling its tokens as a language. Part 9 · 10
2023
DAC (Descript) closes the codebook-usage gap with factorized L2-normalized codes and snake activations; MusicGen generates music directly over EnCodec tokens. Part 9 · 10
2024–25
Single-codebook and semantic codecs (Mimi, WavTokenizer, SpeechTokenizer) trade a little fidelity for far shorter, more predictable token streams — tuned for the language models that consume them. Part 10

→Where to go next

You now have the tokenizer of the neural-audio era: an autoencoder whose discrete bottleneck compresses sound to kilobits and, in the same breath, emits a sequence of integers. The obvious next question is what to do with that sequence — and the answer is to model it exactly as a language.
